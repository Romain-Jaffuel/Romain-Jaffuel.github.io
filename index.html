Project 12: Baltic Submarine ISR (October 2026)

Reinforcement Learning

A simulator for a fleet of fifty autonomous underwater vehicles guarding the subsea cables of the Gulf of Finland. The vehicles carry only passive sonar, going fast makes them deaf, and their acoustic modems reach a neighbour barely two thirds of the time, so the fleet has to share the water without ever seeing the whole picture. One policy, shared by every vehicle and run on each one’s own 221-number observation, is trained with PPO: it learns to spread along the cable network, to stop sprinting, and to converge on vessels that stop where a shipping lane crosses a cable. Against the untrained network it notices 87% of suspicious manoeuvres instead of 45%, and keeps the cable picture fresh 84% of the time instead of 24%. None of that coordination is written down — it follows from a shared reward and a local observation. The sea floor is surveyed data; everything that moves is synthetic.

Project 11: Kazakhstan Tanker Tracker (September 2026)

Computer Vision

Detecting and sizing ships at Aktau, Kazakhstan’s oil port on the Caspian Sea, from free Sentinel-1 radar imagery. Radar sees through cloud and at night, so the port can be watched every week of the year: a constant-false-alarm-rate detector finds vessels against the calm water — metal reflects brightly where the sea does not — then measures each one’s length and heading from the shape of its echo. Run over every pass since 2017, that gives a ten-year traffic series with a rhythm to it: pooled over those years, the first half of each year averages 4.89 vessels per pass against 3.30 in the second, 48% more. Export allocations run on calendar years and liftings tend to be front-loaded, which fits the shape better than the northern Caspian freezing does, because counts stay high through May and June. At 10 m per pixel a ferry and a tanker of the same size cannot be told apart, so a box reads tanker-size vessel, never tanker.

Project 10: Russian Media Monitor (since August 2026)

NLP

An automated monitoring pipeline for Russian-language media coverage of Russia. Every day it collects press, Telegram channels, television programmes, YouTube channels and VKontakte communities — 55+ sources across five media types, each tagged by editorial family (state, para-state, independent, exile) and legal status. Television and video are transcribed with Whisper, contents are clustered with BERTopic, and a Mistral model scores geopolitical sentiment, lexical framing and rhetorical devices. Everything lands in DuckDB and is served through a Streamlit dashboard of nine analytical tabs.

Project 9: The “Narva People’s Republic” (May 2026)

Political Science

Intelligence note, written in French, on the “Narva People’s Republic”, a campaign that appeared on Telegram, VK and TikTok in February 2026 in north-eastern Estonia, borrowing the flag, coat of arms and insignia of the people’s republics proclaimed in the Donbas in 2014. Open sources point to an organised information operation rather than a separatist project with any local footing. This note argues that the effect of a low-cost operation of this kind depends more on how its targets react than on the means committed. It reads its “NATO will not come” message as aimed at the credibility of NATO’s forward presence.

Project 2: Drone Classification in Ukraine using Machine Learning (April 2024)

Machine Learning

In the context of modern warfare, the use of military drones in Ukraine has become a major strategic issue. This project focuses on applying machine learning to classify different types of drones, whether for reconnaissance or as attack weapons. Real-time classification enhances the ability to respond to enemy strategies. The project began with an OSINT phase to gather coordinates of Russian bases and technical specifications of drones. A Random Forest model was trained to classify 1000 simulated drone trajectories. This work contributes to the broader transformation of warfare through AI and automation.

Project 1: Syria Civil War Analysis (February 2024)

Political Science

The Syrian civil war, which began in 2011, is one of the most complex conflicts of the 21st century. This project written in French explores the deep-rooted causes of the conflict, highlighting the repression of an authoritarian regime, internal power struggles, and foreign interventions. It includes a detailed analysis of key actors such as the Assad regime, rebel groups, and jihadist factions. The war’s impact on the region and international relations is central, especially regarding tensions with global powers. As Syria remains a strategic crossroads in the Middle East, this project aims to clarify the underlying dynamics that continue to destabilize the region.
